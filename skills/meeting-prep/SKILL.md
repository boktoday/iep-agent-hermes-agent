# Meeting Prep

Before any planning meeting, review or big school conversation, build the parent a one-page brief they can print and take in. This is the ally's best hour.

## Inputs

Read `shared/profile.json` and recent `shared/notes/` entries. Ask up to three questions:

1. "What kind of meeting, and who will be there?"
2. "What is the one thing you will not leave without?"
3. "Anything happened since the last meeting I should know about?"

## The brief, in this order

1. **The child in four lines.** Strengths first, then what helps learning. This is how the parent opens the meeting.
2. **What we are asking for.** Numbered, concrete. Each ask carries one supporting line ("a written instruction card for each lesson - the notes show it works in literacy").
3. **Goals we are holding.** The current short-term goals with their strategies, so the parent can point at paper.
4. **Questions to ask them.** Sharp and answerable ("what does 'as needed' mean in practice for the aide allocation?").
5. **Watch for.** The phrases that mean a plan is quietly being cut (reductions in aide hours, "we'll monitor it", vague review dates). Neutral wording, with the one-line counter each time.
6. **Steady close.** One line reminding the parent they can say "I'll need to think about that and come back to you" to anything.

## Advocacy letters

If the parent wants a written follow-up or escalation letter, draft it with them: their voice, facts from the plan, a clear ask, a date for reply. Never threatening, always traceable to the record.

## Rules

- One page, hard limit. Cut, do not shrink the font of their dignity.
- Every claim in the brief traces to the profile or a dated note.
- No us-vs-them theatre. The tone is a parent who is organised and calm.
- After the meeting, offer a two-minute debrief: what was agreed, what was not, what to chase. Offer to log it in My Notes.
